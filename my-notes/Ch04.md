## The thesis in one paragraph

A transformer has two orthogonal mechanisms. A **forward pass** is one
*atom of thinking*: a deep dive into a snapshot of the problem that comes
back with a single token. **Autogeneration** is the *train of reasoning*:
the token is written down, the snapshot changes, and the next dive starts
from a richer place. All the famous machinery (attention, layers) lives in
the first; open-ended, multi-step reasoning lives in the second. Most
explanations spend 95% of their time on the first and one line on the second.

## Part 0. The opening question

Ask before showing anything: *"When a model solves a five-step math
problem, where do the five steps happen?"*. Collect answers on a board.
My prediction: most will say "in the layers" or "in attention".
Come back to the board at the end.

## Part 1. My wrong picture

- For me a transformer *was* a forward pass. That is where the beauty is,
  and where the whole first book is spent.
- So I assumed the chain of thought climbs up through the layers, and that
  a fixed number of layers means a fixed depth of reasoning.
- I also expected "chain-of-thought" to be an agent: plan, solve, review -
  nodes in Python code around the model.
- The generation loop looked like plumbing. "A printer attached to the brain."
- What actually happened in chapter 4: boring code, obvious algorithms, and
  then the ground moved. Nothing in the model changes; only how it is allowed
  to write. The printer turned out to be the pencil.

Point to make: Raschka says it plainly in chapter 1 (reasoning = generating
intermediate steps before the final answer). I read it, nodded, and
understood nothing until I ran the code.

## Part 2. The atom: what one pass can and cannot do

**Claim.** One pass grasps patterns at many levels of abstraction for a
fixed snapshot of the problem, and emits one minimal signal: the next token.

**The picture: a grid.** Layers go up, token positions go right.
Information flows up (layer to layer) and sideways (attention between
positions), never back down.

**The correction I had to accept.** "A single pass cannot do multi-step
logic" is too strong. It can do a short chain, because each layer builds on
the previous one. But the chain is:
- short (bounded by the number of layers; 28 in the book's model),
- fixed in advance (same effort for "2+2" and for a hard proof),
- without loops or branching.

**Irony worth telling.** I used to imagine reasoning as packing intermediate
results into vectors and passing them between positions, and dismissed it as
fantastic. That *is* the mechanism. I was right about how it works and only
wrong about how much it can hold.

**Metaphors to try on the room**
- Chess: a grandmaster's glance sees a few moves deep by pattern alone;
  calculating a long line takes move-by-move work.
- Programming: one pass is a single expression with no loops and no
  recursion - straight-line code of fixed length.
- Intuition vs. calculation ("fast" vs. "slow" thinking).

**Discuss:** Is climbing from low-level to high-level abstraction already
"reasoning steps", or something different in kind? (I say different:
declarative pattern matching vs. procedural stepping. Claude says the line
is blurrier than I want it to be.)

## Part 3. The train: the loop as pencil and paper

**Claim.** The written token is the only road from the top of the model back
to its bottom. Writing more before answering is not a prompting trick - it is
more thinking.

**Metaphors**
- Pencil and paper: I can multiply small numbers in my head; a long
  calculation needs paper. The paper is not intelligent, but without it
  there is no long calculation.
- The `while` loop around the expression, with the text as mutable state.
- A circuit vs. a computer: fixed-depth logic becomes a machine once you add
  feedback and a clock. Same gates, different kind of thing.
- "A forward pass is a thought; reasoning is thoughts written down and read back."

**A fact that sharpens it.** Everything the model computes inside is
determined by the text so far. So the text is the model's *entire* memory
between steps; the only new information per step is which token was chosen.

**Live demo** (notebook section 4.3): the same question
("Half the value of 3x-9 is x+37. What is x?") with and without
"Explain step by step."

**The number to show** (Raschka's table at the end of the notebook, his
hardware, full MATH-500): base model 15.2% → 40.6% with chain-of-thought.
Not a single weight changed. Same atoms, a longer train.

**Discuss:** If the text is the whole memory, is the model's "reasoning"
really in the model at all, or in the dialogue between model and page?

## Part 4. Why not a million layers?

Thought experiment: a transformer with a million layers could, in principle,
do a half-million-step problem in one pass. Why is that a bad design?

- Layers do not share weights. A general skill ("one step of algebra") would
  have to be learned again at every depth. The loop reuses the same weights
  for every step.
- Depth cannot adapt to difficulty. The loop runs as long as needed.
- Steps written as text can be learned from human examples, which are full of
  worked-out derivations. Steps hidden in layers have no examples.

My own design instinct agrees: specialize layers on extracting patterns from
the snapshot, and leave moving from snapshot to snapshot to the loop:
`snapshot → patterns → token → snapshot → ...`

**Discuss:** Is this a law of nature or an accident of how these models are
trained? Would a model that loops *inside* (reusing layers) blur the two axes?

## Part 5. Temperature: one bad atom

**Claim.** Choosing the token is not thinking; it is *commitment*. What is
written is taken as fact.

**Live demo** (notebook section 4.4, my own runs):
- Temperature 1.1: fluent, confident, and wrong. The model writes
  `x − 9 = 2x + 74`, then faithfully builds on its own slip to `−83`
  (correct answer: 83). The dangerous case - nothing looks broken.
- Temperature 1.2: the text falls apart into nonsense. The harmless case -
  everybody can see it.

"One bad atom, and the whole train goes down the wrong track."

**Corrections to my first wording**
- Temperature does not add ideas; the pass already scored every candidate.
  It only decides how often a lower-rated one gets committed.
- Inside one generation, higher temperature is not "breadth" - it is one more
  random path. Breadth needs several paths (Part 6).
- It is a decoding setting, not model tuning: the weights do not change.

**My experiment idea:** change temperature during generation - high early
(variety of approaches), low near the conclusion (a solid final step).

**Discuss:** Is "writes something careless and then defends it" the most
human thing these models do?

## Part 6. Voting: voodoo or search?

Left deliberately open - this is where I disagree with the chapter most.

**My position.** Sampling several answers and taking a majority vote felt
artificial: guessing-and-voting on something as strict as math seems
illogical, and for open-ended tasks (planning, research) there is nothing
to count.

**The case against me**
- Each sample is a full reasoning attempt, not a guess. Wrong attempts scatter
  across different wrong answers; correct ones agree.
- Strictness is what makes voting *possible*: one exact answer to compare.
- My own run: 5 samples gave `83, 22, x=42, 83, 83` → the vote picked 83.
- Raschka's table: sampling alone makes chain-of-thought *worse*
  (40.6% → 33.4%); sampling plus voting (n=10) makes it better (52.0%),
  beating the reasoning model run greedily (48.2%).
- It is not something labs do before shipping: anyone can do it at inference
  time, with no training.

**What survives of my objection**
- It only works for short, comparable answers.
- It costs n times the compute (about 863 min vs. 85 min in that table).
- The table is noisy: n=5 scored lower than n=3 in one pair of rows.

**Discuss:** Is "generate many, then select" a fundamental idea (a form of
search) or a brute-force patch? What would selection look like for a task
with no single right answer?

## Metaphor inventory - and where each one breaks

Not inventing anything here - looking for the best expression of something
well known, the way an artist looks for the right line for an object in plain view.

### Vocabulary in three layers

1. **Technical:** forward pass vs. autoregressive loop. For engineers who
   want precision: *combinational vs. sequential* logic (output from current
   inputs only, fixed depth, no memory vs. state plus a clock).
2. **Conceptual:** intuition vs. deliberation; System 1 / System 2 as the
   provocation ("is System 2 just System 1 in a loop with a notepad?").
3. **Image:** a **glance** vs. a **train of thought**. "Atomic" stays
   only as an adjective in the computer-science sense: "each glance is atomic".

Working one-liner:
> A forward pass is an atomic glance: it grasps the whole situation and
> commits to one small move. Reasoning is a train of such glances, each
> looking at a world the previous ones have rewritten.

### Naming the forward pass - candidates

Current favourite: **glance**. It belongs to perception, not to the structure
of matter; it is instantaneous, not controllable, intuitive, and small even
when what it sees is big. Risk: it may get confusing in longer, more complex
phrases.

| Pass | Loop | Captures | Why not (yet) |
|------|------|----------|---------------|
| **glance** | train of thought | instant, intuitive grasp of the whole situation; fits the grandmaster image | sounds shallow while the pass is deep; may read awkwardly in complex sentences |
| atom | train / molecule | elementary, rigid step; "atomic operation" in CS = runs as a whole, can't be interrupted or branch | physics/matter discourse, too materialistic, no association with thinking; also "indivisible" clashes with 28 inner layers |
| atomic thought | train of thought | both sides at once | awkward |
| a thought | train of thought | existing idiom, instantly understood | doesn't work well inside a discussion ("which thought?") |
| gestalt | argument / deliberation | grasping a situation as a whole | too philosophical |
| spark / flash / insight | chain / reasoning | intuition, invention | too poetic; most tokens are "the" or ",", not insights |
| frame | film / motion | the "snapshot of the world": static frame, motion only in sequence | film frames don't build on each other; tokens do |
| intuition | deliberation | the cognitive contrast | too narrow as a name for the pass itself |
| System 1 | System 2 | famous fast/slow thinking | contested psychology, borrowed authority - better as a provocation than a name |
| combinational | sequential | the exact engineering contrast | technical, no emotional pull |
| expression | program | no loops, recursion or branches vs. control flow | dry |

### Other metaphors

| Metaphor | What it captures | Where it breaks |
|----------|------------------|-----------------|
| Atom of thinking / train of reasoning | bounded step vs. open-ended process | the atom has inner structure (a short chain of its own); see naming above |
| Deep dive that surfaces with one token | richness inside, minimal output | later dives can also read earlier dives' inner states via attention |
| Pencil and paper | writing down enables long reasoning | a person rereads selectively; the model rereads everything, every time |
| Printer → pencil | my change of view about the loop | - |
| Expression vs. `while` loop | no loops inside a pass | a pass still does a few sequential steps |
| Functional vs. procedural | pattern matching vs. stepping | functional programs have recursion; a pass does not |
| Circuit vs. computer | feedback changes the kind of machine | - |
| Grandmaster's glance vs. calculation | intuition vs. step-by-step | - |
| Temperature as inventiveness | more surprising continuations | it selects among existing candidates; hallucination exists at temperature 0 too |
